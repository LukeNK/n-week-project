### Special cellular structure
In plants, the cells are a bit different compared to animal cells. The first difference is the existence of cell wall, which can be splitted into two parts:
- **primary cell wall**: a thin outer layer of cellulose that provides flexible structural support; and
- **secondary cell wall**: a thick rigid layer of cellulose mixed with lignin that provides rigid structural support.

Because the lignin does not allow water to pass through, the cells need small channels called **plasmodesmata** to allow cell-to-cell transport. Finally, the cells are also gluded together with a pectin coating called **middle lamella** outside of the primary cell wall.

It is quite costly to make cytosol fluid, so plant cells "cheat" their way in order to appear bigger by filling water into the central vacuole. That will create the **turgor pressure** against the cell wall to provide structural support. So when we say a cell is **turgid**, it is mean that the cell is inflated to the point it can fill the entire space within the cell wall, while **flaccid** will mean the opposite.

### Plant tissue
Generally, a **tissue** means a group of cells that serve a similar purposes. In plants, biologists group the cells into three main tissues from outer-most layer into the centre of the plant as: dermal tissue, ground tissue, and vascular tissue.

#### Dermal tissue
A "dermatologist" is a doctor that can treat your skin, so a dermal tissue is essentially the "skin" of a plant. Our journey starts with the **epidermal tissue** that forms the outer-most layer of plants, where waxy cuticle are being produced (just imagine it like your sweat). Then we have the **guard cell** which forms the lips of the stomata to control the intake of air and the loss of fluid from plant.

#### Ground tissue
The cells here are given some really cool names that are not so cool to remember before your tests. **Parenchyma** is a living cell that conduct most of the metabolism function. Essentially, you want to put the "factory" near the "resource", so this cell is usually located in the leaf. We then have **collenchyma** that provide flexible support in growing plat structure. Lastly, we have **sclerenchyma** cells that are dead at maturity. This is because their secondary cell walls have gotten too think to allow water to pass through; but also because of that, they act as a rigid structural support.

#### Vascular tissue
This is the system that allows plants to transport resources across their body. There are two different "highways routes" in plants:
- xylem, which is responsible for the transportation of water up the plant; and
- phloem, which is responsible to move sugar across the plant.

**Xylem** is a system that mostly comprised of dead cells. Here, there are two "types" of highways:
- **Trachieds**: These vertically long cells are hollow in the middle with thick secondary cell wall on the outside. There are pits on the side between the cells to enable water to "climb" through the cell-stairs if it wants to go up.
- **Transport vessles**: These cells are shorter than trachieds but they have perforation plates at the top and bottom of the cells. This allows water to just go straight to the top like an elevator.

**Phloem** has cells that are alive but are specialized in their function, so they are a bit different. There is also two different types of cells:
- **Sieve tube elements**: These cells are alive at maturity, but they have no nucleus, ribosomes, or vacuoles. Just like transport vessles, they are arrached end-to-end with sieve plates between the cells.
- **Companion cells**: These cells are connected to the sieve tube elements through plasmodesmata to keep them alive. Their purpose is to maintain the highway for transporting sugar.

### Plant systems and growth
So plants are splitted into two parts:
- **Root**: This is the part underground that are responsible for the taking up water and nutrient. It also have the obvious function of anchoring the plant.
- **Shoot**: This is the part above the ground which can capture light for photosynthesis. It is also where sexual reproduction happens.
